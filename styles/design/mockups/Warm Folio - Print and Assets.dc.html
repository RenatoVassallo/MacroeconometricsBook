5.3 The Minnesota prior

A VAR with n = 7 variables and p = 4 lags carries 203 coefficients per equation block, against 100 usable quarterly observations. Write the reduced form as

yt = c + A1yt−1 + ⋯ + Apyt−p + ut,  ut ~ N(0, Σ)
(5.1)

and stack the T observations, so that least squares has the closed form B̂ = (X′X)−1X′Y in (5.2). Shrinkage buys precision at the cost of a little bias, and the prior below is the standard way to spend it (Litterman, 1986).

Assumption 5.1 · Recursive identification
ut = B0−1εt, with B0−1 lower triangular and 𝔼[εtε′t] = In, so that Σ = B0−1B0−1′.
Definition 5.1 · Minnesota prior
Prior mean δi on the own first lag of variable i and zero on all other lag coefficients, with prior variance (5.3).
Intuition
The prior shrinks each equation toward a univariate process: λ1 sets overall tightness, λ2 how much other variables' lags may matter, λ3 how fast distant lags are switched off.
Algorithm 5.1 · Gibbs sampler
01Draw vec(B) | Σ, Y ~ N(b̄, V̄), V̄ = (V0−1 + Σ−1 ⊗ X′X)−1.
02Draw Σ | B, Y ~ 𝒥𝒲(S0 + (Y − XB)′(Y − XB), ν0 + T).
B_ols = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ Y) U = Y - X @ B_ols Sigma_ols = U.T @ U / (T - k)
1 Bańbura, Giannone and Reichlin (2010) scale λ1 with the cross-section size; Giannone, Lenza and Primiceri (2015) treat it as a hyperparameter with its own prior.
Margin note. Litterman's prior treated Σ as fixed and diagonal. Algorithm 5.1 relaxes both, at the cost of the closed-form posterior.
